The 400 Person Work Experiment

Researchers pulled together a ecosystem of 400 real online workers to isolate statistical bias. To clear pre-existing identity judgments, they split them into two simple squads: Team A and Team B.

But there was an intentional twist: Team A was a small minority making up just 20% of the market (80 people). Team B was a massive crowd making up 80% of the market (320 people).

Look at the graphic grid—each colored dot perfectly outlines the starting baseline layout of the candidate market pool.

Team A Minority: 20% Team B Majority: 80%

The Backwards Typing Test

Every single worker sat down to complete an intensive task: look at jumbled alphanumeric text strings, reverse them mentally, and type them in perfectly under high-speed constraints.

Go ahead and test your speed in the emulator above! It handles lowercase or uppercase effortlessly, and auto-resets immediately if you type a character wrong.

The Equal Result: When the metrics were averaged, both groups performed perfectly identically. Both teams successfully reversed an average of exactly 16 strings. Neither group held an inherent skill advantage.

The Deceptive Top 5 Elite List

When looking exclusively at the extreme high-performers—the absolute top 5 highest scorers out of all 400 participants—something highly deceptive emerged.

Because Team B's population pool is 4x larger, sample size laws dictate that Team B naturally claims almost all the top spots by pure probability.

How This Replicates the Research Paper

In the original study, participants were given identical parameters using neutral labels to completely isolate objective statistical logic from cultural stereotyping.

The Psychological Breakdown: When hiring managers see that majority Team B dominates the elite high-performer list, they fall into a cognitive loop called Base Rate Neglect. They completely overlook that Team B was a massive crowd from the beginning.

They misread the elite list as structural proof that Team B is inherently more talented. In the experiment, this statistical error triggered a severe **10 percentage point penalty in selection rates** against perfectly equal minority candidates.
